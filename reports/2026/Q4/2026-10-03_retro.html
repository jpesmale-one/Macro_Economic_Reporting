THE VERDICT, BOTH HALVES The set did its job and the board did not. Four of four pairs travelled at least 0.75 of a normal day in the called direction, three of them on multiple sessions. Underneath it, EUR was ranked second at +6.00 and finished dead last on realised strength.

Three of four pairs are short AUD. AUD fell, and that single read carried the week. The method has still not been tested on a week where its concentrated leg went the wrong way — 14–18 Sep was that test, and it went 0-for-3.

1 — The report build

1a. What went well?

R-46 ran first and stopped the build twice, correctly both times — six rows from the prior week had not been loaded. That is the checklist doing what it was built for after F-132.

LD-146 and LD-147 both shipped inside the week they were asked for, with tests, and neither broke anything downstream. R-44 went 31 → 45 cases.

The AUD employment split was load-bearing for the first time. 39.5K (FT −6.3K, PT +45.8K) graded BAD on the full-time leg per LD-57, and AUD did finish weak. One observation, not a proof — but it is the first live week where the F-138 fix mattered.

1b. What problems did we see and resolve?

FindingWhat it was
F-140R-46 step 9 was reading LAST WEEK’S PAGE. Hardcoded 2026-09-20_weekly.html. It passed on V1 by coincidence, then failed against a correct page once values were refreshed. A guard that names the wrong file does not weaken to a no-op — it accuses correct work.
The rate row printed a dashThe engine scored the RBA −1 while the page showed — in both the Threshold and Score columns. Three separate causes, all live on V3.
raw[-3:] read “ION” The collision table took the last three characters of the event name as its reference month, so Interest Rate Decision printed ION — on the one row LD-146 exists to explain.
Wrong rule name, correct scoreThe collision label was a two-way if/else that could only say RULE 1 or RULE 2, so rate precedence rendered as “same month, shared” when the decision had taken the day outright.
THE THROUGH-LINE All four are DISPLAY defects on CORRECT data. The model knew the right answer every time and the page said something else. That is a new failure class here — the previous twelve findings were mostly wrong values — and it is harder to catch because no value check fires on it.

1c. What can we improve?

1. Rate rows keep going invisible to the display layer. Three findings in two weeks — hot() in F-135, precedence in LD-146, the threshold column this week — one cause: rate rows live in rate_model / week_ahead while nearly every renderer resolves through score_model. A row-level lookup that knows both would kill the class instead of the instances.

2. rate_model is hand-written and nothing checks it against TE. The RBA hiked to 4.60% on 29 Sep; the cell still reads 4.35%. R-46 step 6 verifies the page matches rate_model — never that rate_model matches the world. All eight rates could drift and every guard would stay green. R-47 is the fix.

3. Two R-45 slugs point at the wrong series. CB Consumer Confidence → University of Michigan (a different survey); NZD Card Spending YoY → levels in NZD millions. Both report NO PAGE, and that is the only reason neither has produced a bad value — had either parsed, R-45 would have flagged our correct 89.4 as stale against Michigan’s 48.1.

4. The §4 terms column describes a retired model. It prints expected +0.50 + surprise +0.00; LD-142 retired both in favour of realised 0.2 · level 0.4 · path 0.4. The scores are right and the working beside them is a model we no longer run. A stale explanation on a correct number cannot be caught by a value check.

5. The pair_swap.LIVE overlay must die at the next build. Once rate_model holds 4.60%, the AUD entry has to go — an overlay that outlives its discrepancy becomes a second source of truth (F-127’s shape).

1d. What do we continue?

Running R-46 first every Sunday — it has stopped a bad build three weeks running. Writing the retrospective to the repo before publishing (LD-138). Recording a withdrawal condition with every new rule, so a rule that ranks nothing comes out instead of accumulating. And doubting the check when it produces an implausible finding — F-134 taught that; F-140 was the one-finding version and I still doubted the page first.

THE 21–25 SEP ROW IS THE CORRECTED ONE That retrospective was first graded on Yahoo bars and republished on OANDA (F-137); the regrade moved base rho +0.29 → +0.48. Quoting the superseded figure here would have made this week look like an improvement on the base when it is the opposite.

So the base FELL this week, +0.48 → +0.31, and it still beats the final for the second week running. LD-144 cut the calibrator to 0.2/day precisely because it was dragging the ranking down, and it is still dragging — just less. The calibrator has never once improved the ranking in five weeks.

⚠ NONE OF THIS IS STATISTICALLY MEANINGFUL With n=8, rho needs roughly ±0.71 for p<0.05. Every reading in that table is indistinguishable from chance — the 4 Sep retrospective said so first. Five weeks of consistent direction is worth watching; it is not evidence, and treating +0.31 as “better than +0.26” would be reading noise.

EUR is the single biggest ranking error. Ranked second at +6.00, finished dead last at −0.58%. Nothing in the macro base saw it coming — worth a specific look before Sunday rather than a general resolution to do better.

The rule was right about direction — AUD was weak and the hike did not rescue it. But it ranked nothing, and LD-113 and LD-140 are both precedents for a rule withdrawn on exactly that. One week is not a verdict; 12–16 Oct decides it, and the condition is already recorded.

LD-147’s first outing was informative in a way I did not expect. All four pairs carried negative swap, and the reason is structural: the board ranks on macro strength, and this week the strong currencies (GBP 3.75%, EUR 2.65%, JPY 1.25%) all carry lower policy rates than the weak one (AUD 4.60%). A macro-divergence model will systematically select carry-negative positions. Not a defect, not an argument against the set — but a standing property of the method, and it only became visible once the column existed.

2d. What best practices do we continue?

Grading on the day’s range, not the close — EURAUD is this week’s proof. Disclosing leg overlap as concentration rather than dressing it as diversification (F-128). Naming the event risk before the week starts — the RBA warning was on the page Sunday, so Tuesday was not a surprise. And publishing the five-column grid, not a win/loss count: AUDJPY hit on 3 of 5 days and GBPAUD on 2, which a weekly figure cannot tell apart.

What would change my mind

On the calibrator: if 5–9 Oct shows the final beating the base, LD-144’s weighting is working and the trend reverses. If the base wins a third time, the calibrator needs a harder look than a weight change.

On LD-146: if it changes a pairing by 16 Oct, it stays. If it has still ranked nothing, it comes out — LD-113’s precedent.

On the set: four from four is a good week by the measure we chose. It is also one AUD read expressed three times, and a week where AUD had risen would have read very differently. The method has still not been tested on a week where its concentrated leg went the wrong way — 14–18 Sep was that test, and it went 0-for-3.
